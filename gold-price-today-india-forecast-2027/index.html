Gold has crashed 30% from its record. What happens next?

MarketsOctober 2026Vitt-Marg Advisors

In January 2026 gold did something no one alive had seen: it crossed $5,500 an ounce. On 28 January it closed at a record of about $5,589, and intraday feeds touched roughly $5,608. Nine months later, on 30 October, spot gold slipped below $3,900 — its lowest level of the year and roughly 30% below that peak. The metal that was supposed to be "safe" has had one of the wildest years in its modern history.

So what is going on? Why does gold fall when there is a war in the Middle East, oil is above $110 and inflation is rising — the very conditions that are supposed to help it? This guide walks through today's prices, the real reasons behind the swings, the viral "mercury-to-gold" story, three scenarios for the next twelve months, and how an Indian investor can think about buying now.

Two things stand out. First, the Indian price fell less than the dollar price over the month, because the 15% import duty and the exchange rate cushion local prices. Second, the fall has been steep: 24K retail gold was near ₹1,49,300 as recently as 21 October.

MCX, spot and jewellery prices are three different things

  • International spot gold is the price of gold for immediate delivery, quoted in US dollars per troy ounce (31.1 g). The LBMA Gold Price, set twice a day in London, is the global benchmark.
  • MCX gold futures are rupee contracts for future delivery, priced per 10 g. They include the import duty and the rupee-dollar rate. Early on 30 October, the December contract traded near ₹1,41,902, down 0.49%, before international prices fell further later that day.
  • Jewellery (retail) rates follow MCX and spot, but add dealer margins and, at the billing counter, 3% GST and making charges.

A simple rule of thumb links them: ₹ per 10 g ≈ US$ per ounce × USD/INR ÷ 3.11 × 1.15 (the last factor is the 15% duty). At about ₹95 to the dollar, every $100 move in spot gold moves the Indian price by roughly ₹3,500 per 10 g.

Why are gold prices falling — and swinging so violently?

Gold does not pay interest. Its price is therefore a tug-of-war between fear (which pushes it up) and the return investors give up by holding it (which pushes it down). In 2026, the second force has been winning. Here is how each factor is working right now.

1. The Federal Reserve has started raising rates again

The Fed raised its policy rate by 25 basis points on 16 September 2026, the first hike of this cycle, and on 28 October it held the range at 3.75%–4.00% in a 10–2 vote, with two members wanting another hike. Futures markets now put a high probability on a further hike in December. When rates rise, bonds and deposits pay more, so the opportunity cost of holding gold goes up. That is the single biggest reason for this year's slide.

2. Treasury yields at multi-decade highs and a firmer dollar

The 10-year US Treasury yield closed at about 5.05% on 28 October, after touching 5.31% on 5 October — levels last seen around 2006–07. Gold is priced in dollars, so a stronger dollar makes it costlier for buyers elsewhere, and high real (inflation-adjusted) yields make a zero-yield asset less attractive. JPMorgan Private Bank expects yields could peak around 5.25%–5.50% and sees gold finding "a solid bottom when rates peak."

3. Inflation and oil: why war is not helping gold this time

Normally, conflict lifts gold. This year, the Middle East crisis has instead pushed oil sharply higher — Brent was near $116 a barrel on 30 October — and US inflation has followed: September CPI was 3.7% year-on-year and PCE inflation 3.6%. Higher inflation means a more hawkish Fed, and a more hawkish Fed hurts gold. In other words, the geopolitical shock is reaching gold through interest rates, not through safe-haven buying.

4. Central banks are still buying — and that is the floor

Official-sector demand remains the market's backbone. The World Gold Council (WGC) reported central-bank purchases of about 289 tonnes in Q2 2026, and China's central bank added about 63 tonnes in Q3, extending a buying streak of 23 consecutive months. The WGC says net purchases have averaged around 1,000 tonnes a year over the last four years. Central banks buy slowly and rarely sell into falls, which is why many analysts expect them to cushion deeper declines.

5. ETF and institutional flows cut both ways

Gold ETFs saw a record US$31 billion of inflows in Q3 2026, according to the WGC. But leveraged and fast money is fickle: October's slide has been amplified by ETF outflows, speculative selling and a sell-off in technology stocks that forced some investors to sell gold to meet margin calls. In India, gold ETFs took in ₹4,450 crore in September — a seven-month high — as investors bought the dip.

6. Profit booking and MCX speculation

After gold more than doubled between 2023 and January 2026, many holders were sitting on large gains. When the trend broke, profit booking turned orderly declines into sharp ones. On MCX, average daily volume in September was about 18 tonnes, and leveraged positions magnify every move in both directions.

7. The rupee and India's 15% import duty

For Indian buyers, two local factors matter as much as the global price. The government raised the effective import duty on gold from 6% to 15% from 13 May 2026 to curb imports and support the rupee, which had weakened past 95 to the dollar by May. A weaker rupee raises local gold prices; a stronger one lowers them. Over the past month the rupee has firmed slightly to about 95, which is part of why Indian prices fell a little more than you would expect from the duty alone. India's gold imports in September fell 81% year-on-year to about US$1.8 billion.

The mercury-to-gold story: can science actually make gold?

Social media has been buzzing with claims that scientists can now "turn mercury into gold", with some posts suggesting gold prices could crash. Here is what is actually true.

Yes, it is physically possible. Changing one element into another is called nuclear transmutation: you change the number of protons in an atom's nucleus, usually by hitting it with neutrons or other particles. Mercury (80 protons) sits right next to gold (79 protons) in the periodic table. Physicists at Harvard produced radioactive gold isotopes from mercury as early as 1941, and in May 2025 CERN's ALICE experiment reported in Physical Review C that lead collisions in the Large Hadron Collider had created about 86 billion gold nuclei between 2015 and 2018 — a total of roughly 29 picograms, or 0.000000000029 grams, each nucleus surviving only a fraction of a second.

The new claim. In July 2025, US fusion start-up Marathon Fusion posted a preprint (arXiv:2507.13461, not yet peer-reviewed at the time) proposing that neutrons from future deuterium–tritium fusion power plants could convert mercury-198 into mercury-197, which then decays into stable gold-197. Its simulations estimate more than 2 tonnes of gold per gigawatt of thermal power per year. On 21 October 2026 the company said experiments had produced gold at a rate ten times CERN's record. Those results have not been independently verified.

Fact check: why this will not crash gold prices any time soon

  • No commercial fusion plant exists yet. The scheme depends on gigawatt-scale fusion reactors that are still years, probably decades, from routine operation.
  • The scale is tiny next to mining. Mines produce roughly 3,600 tonnes a year. Even at 2 tonnes per gigawatt, matching just 1% of mine supply would need about 18 gigawatts of fusion capacity.
  • The gold comes out radioactive. Other gold isotopes form too, so the metal would need to be stored for many years — about 17–18 years, by one estimate — before it could be handled like normal gold.
  • Feedstock is limited. Mercury-198 makes up only about 10% of natural mercury, and isotope enrichment adds cost.

Verdict: a genuine and fascinating piece of nuclear physics, and a possible by-product for future fusion plants, but not a realistic factor for gold prices in the next several years.

No one can predict gold prices reliably, and anyone promising a "target" should be treated with caution. What we can do is set out what would have to happen for gold to go up, sideways or down. Bull case: what could push gold higher

The Fed stops hiking after December and signals cuts in 2027 as growth slows; Treasury yields fall back below 4.5%; the dollar weakens; central banks keep buying at the pace of recent years; and the Middle East conflict escalates in a way that hurts growth more than it lifts inflation. Recession fears would also revive safe-haven demand.

Base case: the most reasonable path

One more Fed hike, then a long pause; yields stay high but stop rising; central-bank buying puts a floor under prices; and gold spends the next few months building a base between roughly $3,800 and $4,400 before gradually recovering towards the analysts' median as rates peak. For Indian investors, a stable rupee and unchanged 15% duty would keep local prices broadly in line with the dollar price.

Bear case: what could trigger a deeper correction

Inflation keeps surprising on the upside and the Fed hikes several more times; yields push towards 5.5%; ETF holders and speculators keep selling; central banks slow their purchases at higher prices; and a stronger rupee or a duty cut lowers Indian prices further. A clean break below the $3,886 support would likely bring in more technical selling.

Is this the right time to buy gold?

Prices are about 30% below their peak, which tempts many buyers. But a lower price is not the same as a cheap price, and the forces that pushed gold down — high rates and a hawkish Fed — have not gone away yet. A sensible approach depends on why you want gold.

  • As a long-term diversifier: many financial planners suggest keeping gold to around 5–15% of a portfolio. Gold has historically held value when equities and the currency come under stress, which is its main job.
  • Staggered, not lump-sum: with volatility this high, buying in instalments over 6–12 months (for example, a monthly SIP in a gold ETF or fund) reduces the risk of buying everything at a bad moment.
  • Avoid leverage: trading MCX futures in this market can wipe out capital quickly.

Frequently asked questions

What is the gold price today in India?

On 31 October 2026, indicative 24K gold was about ₹1,42,450 per 10 grams, 22K about ₹1,31,140 and 18K about ₹1,07,375, excluding 3% GST and making charges. Rates vary slightly by city and jeweller.

Why are gold prices falling in 2026?

Mainly because US interest rates are rising. The Fed hiked in September 2026 and markets expect another hike in December, while 10-year Treasury yields are near 5%. Higher yields and a firmer dollar raise the cost of holding gold, which pays no interest. ETF outflows and profit booking after the January record have added to the fall.

Why is gold falling despite the war in the Middle East?

The conflict has pushed oil prices above $110 a barrel, which raises inflation and makes the Fed more likely to keep rates high. That interest-rate effect has outweighed safe-haven buying.

What is the gold price prediction for 2027?

No one can predict it reliably. A Reuters poll of 30 analysts in October 2026 put the median 2027 forecast at about $4,650 an ounce. Our illustrative 12-month base case is $4,000–4,600, roughly ₹1.40–1.61 lakh per 10 grams at today's exchange rate and duty.

Can mercury really be turned into gold?

Yes, through nuclear transmutation, and it has been done in laboratories. But today's methods produce tiny or unverified amounts, depend on fusion plants that do not yet exist commercially, and create radioactive gold that must be stored for years. It is not expected to affect gold prices in the foreseeable future.

Should I buy gold now or wait?

If gold fits your long-term asset allocation, buying gradually over 6–12 months is generally safer than investing a lump sum in such a volatile market. Avoid leverage, and choose regulated, low-cost routes such as gold ETFs.

Are Sovereign Gold Bonds still available in 2026?

No new SGB tranche has been issued since February 2024. Existing bonds trade on the stock exchanges, but from 1 April 2026 the tax-free maturity benefit applies only to original subscribers who hold until maturity.

The bottom line

  • Gold's 2026 fall is mainly about interest rates: the Fed is hiking, Treasury yields are near 5%, and oil-driven inflation is keeping them there.
  • Central banks are still buying, which is why many analysts expect a floor rather than a collapse.
  • In India, the 15% import duty and the rupee matter almost as much as the global price.
  • The mercury-to-gold research is real science, but it has no realistic effect on prices in the foreseeable future.
  • If you want gold, decide your allocation first, then buy in instalments through low-cost, regulated routes.

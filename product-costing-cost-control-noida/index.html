Why per-unit cost matters

Your profit and loss account shows one profit for the whole business. It cannot tell you that one product earns well while another loses money on every unit sold. Per-unit costing separates the two, so you can decide what to push, what to reprice and what to stop making.

Contribution and break-even, with an example

The first step is to split costs into variable costs, which rise with every unit you make, and fixed costs, which you pay anyway, like rent, salaries and loan interest.

ParticularsAmount
Selling price per unit₹250
Variable cost per unit (material, power, packing, freight)₹170
Contribution per unit₹80
Fixed costs per month₹12,00,000
Break-even volume (₹12,00,000 ÷ ₹80)15,000 units a month
Current sales20,000 units a month
Margin of safety5,000 units, or 25% of sales

This tells the owner three useful things. Every unit above 15,000 adds ₹80 to profit. A price cut of ₹20 a unit would push break-even up to 20,000 units, which is the whole of current sales. And an order at ₹200 a unit still adds ₹30 of contribution per unit if the factory has spare capacity, even though it looks like a loss on a full-cost basis.

What we set up

  • Per-unit cost for each product, built from your bill of materials, actual purchase rates, labour time, power use and a fair share of overheads.
  • Contribution and break-even analysis by product and by customer, so you can see who and what pays for the fixed costs.
  • Standard costing and variance analysis: a standard cost for each product, and a monthly report of where actual costs went off track, split into price, usage, rate and efficiency.
  • Budgetary control: a monthly budget for each department, with actuals compared every month.
  • Inventory control: ABC analysis, reorder levels and economic order quantities, so cash is not stuck in slow stock.
  • Wastage and yield tracking: scrap, rejection and process loss by machine or shift, which is often where the quickest savings are.
  • Decision support: make or buy, accept or reject a low-price order, add a shift, or drop a product.

Where savings usually come from

  • Material yield and scrap, which in many Noida factories is two to five percent more than it needs to be.
  • Power cost per unit, especially from idle running and poor power factor.
  • Products priced on an old cost that has since gone up.
  • Small customers who take a lot of handling, freight and credit for very little contribution.

Frequently asked questions

We are a small factory. Is this only for large companies?

No. Smaller manufacturers often benefit most, because one loss-making product or one leaking process can wipe out a large share of their profit. The work is scaled to the size of your business.

What is the difference between a cost sheet and cost control?

A cost sheet tells you what a product cost in a period. Cost control is the monthly system that compares actual cost with a standard and acts on the difference, so the cost keeps coming down rather than just being reported.

Do you need access to our accounting software?

Read-only access or a monthly export of the trial balance, purchase register and stock reports is enough. We also need production data such as quantities, machine hours and scrap, which your production team usually already keeps.

How soon will we see results?

Most clients see the first clear findings, such as a product priced below cost or an unusual scrap level, within the first month. Savings depend on how quickly the business acts on them.

No more arrest for unpaid income tax: what CBDT Notification 120/2026 changes

Income TaxOctober 2026Vitt-Marg Advisors

Until this year, a Tax Recovery Officer could issue an arrest warrant against a person who did not pay an income-tax demand and send them to civil prison. That power has now gone. On 17 September 2026, CBDT issued Notification No. 120/2026 (the Income-tax (Fourth Amendment) Rules, 2026). It removes arrest and detention from the recovery rules, with effect from 1 April 2026.

This does not mean unpaid tax has no consequences. The department's other recovery powers are untouched, and prosecution for hiding income or keeping TDS is still very much alive. Here is what changed, what didn't, and what to do if you have a demand pending.

What exactly changed

  • Parliament first. The Finance Act, 2026 removed arrest and detention from the modes of recovery in both the Income-tax Act, 1961 and the Income-tax Act, 2025. The explanatory memorandum gave a one-line reason: "other modes of recovery are considered sufficient."
  • Then the rules. Notification No. 120/2026 amended Rule 225 of the Income-tax Rules, 2026, which sets out the recovery procedure. It deleted the sub-rules on arrest warrants, bringing the defaulter before the Tax Recovery Officer, the detention inquiry and civil-prison orders, and the references to arrest elsewhere in the rule.
  • Retrospective. The rule change applies from 1 April 2026, the day the new Act came into force. Any arrest-based recovery step started since then has no legal basis.

This change is about recovery of tax that is due. It does not remove arrest in criminal prosecution under the Income-tax Act, and it does not touch arrest powers under other laws such as GST.

What the department can still do if you don't pay

Recovery stepWhat it means in practice
Attachment of bank accounts and money owed to you (Section 416)A notice to your bank, employer, customer or tenant to pay your tax dues directly to the department. A person who ignores the notice can be treated as a defaulter for that amount.
Attachment and sale of movable propertyVehicles, stock, shares and other movable assets can be attached and auctioned.
Attachment and sale of immovable propertyLand, flats and buildings can be attached, and sold if the demand stays unpaid.
Appointment of a receiverA receiver can be appointed to manage a property and collect income from it towards the arrears.
Adjustment of refundsRefunds due for other years can be set off against the pending demand.

For most businesses, the bank-account notice is the real risk. It can freeze working capital within days, and banks act on it immediately.

Not paying versus hiding: prosecution still applies

The law now treats two situations differently:

  • You can't pay. This is a recovery problem. The department will go after your assets, not your liberty.
  • You hid income, or deducted TDS and kept it. This is an offence. Prosecution for wilful attempts to evade tax, and for failing to deposit TDS or TCS that you collected, continues under the Income-tax Act. On conviction, these offences carry imprisonment.

So the change protects the honest defaulter who has a cash-flow problem or a disputed demand. It does nothing for someone who moved money to avoid paying.

Why the change makes sense

  • The Constitution. In Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980), the Supreme Court held that jailing a person merely because they cannot pay a debt, without any element of bad faith, violates the right to life and liberty under Article 21.
  • Coercion during searches. In Radhika Agarwal v. Union of India (2025), the Supreme Court made clear that tax officers cannot use the threat of arrest to force payment. Any payment has to be voluntary.
  • Practice. Arrest for income-tax arrears was used very rarely. Bank attachment and property attachment already did the work.

The other side of the argument

  • Arrest was a last resort against defaulters who have money but have hidden it out of reach. Without it, recovery from such people depends on tracing assets, which takes time.
  • GST is different. Under Section 69 of the CGST Act, a GST officer can still arrest a person for specified serious offences, such as fake invoices or tax collected but not paid beyond the monetary limit. The Supreme Court upheld this power in 2025 but required strict safeguards. So the same business owner can face arrest under GST but not under income tax.
  • State laws. Some state revenue laws still allow arrest and detention for arrears recovered as land revenue. That leaves a gap between how the Centre now treats income-tax defaulters and how other dues are recovered.

If you have an income-tax demand pending

  1. Check it on the portal. Log in to the e-filing portal, go to Pending Actions → Response to Outstanding Demand, and see the year, amount and order behind it.
  2. Find out if it is right. Many demands come from TDS credit not matching, a processing error or a wrong set-off. These can often be fixed through rectification rather than an appeal.
  3. Agree or disagree on the portal. If you disagree, say so with reasons in the demand response. This stops automatic adjustment of your refunds against it.
  4. Appeal and ask for a stay if the demand comes from an assessment you dispute. A stay application, often with part payment, keeps recovery action on hold while the appeal is decided.
  5. Pay in instalments if the demand is correct but you can't pay at once. Ask the Assessing Officer before recovery starts, not after your bank account is attached.

The worst option is to ignore the demand. Arrest may be gone, but a frozen bank account in the middle of a GST or salary cycle can hurt a business more.
